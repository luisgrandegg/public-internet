# The Internet Belongs to Us

### A manifesto for technology that gives power back to people

---

**The promise was that we could deal with each other directly. Instead, we got new middlemen.**

Networked technology was supposed to remove the gatekeepers. For a moment, it looked like it would. Then a handful of platforms quietly inserted themselves *between* us — between a diner and the kitchen, a traveler and a host, a shopper and the shop on their own street. They didn't cook the food, build the restaurant, or own the spare room. They built the layer in the middle. And then they charged rent on it.

Every transaction, a cut. Every connection, tracked and sold. The value created by millions of people coordinating their ordinary lives ended up concentrated in the hands of a very few. We were told this was the price of convenience. It was never the price. It was the business model.

It did not have to go this way. It does not have to stay this way.

The coordination layer — the software that lets people find each other, pool what they have, and decide things together — is just code. It can be a commons. It can belong to the communities that use it. We are living in the most powerful technological moment in human history. The tools to build the alternative are cheaper and more accessible than they have ever been. So let's build it, and let's get it right this time.

---

## What we believe

**We build tools, not platforms.** Our job is the coordination layer — and then to hand over the keys. We do not want to become the next intermediary collecting rent on other people's work. Our success is measured by how little we are needed once a tool is in a community's hands.

**The people who coordinate should own the coordination.** Cooperatives, neighborhood associations, small merchants, and municipalities should govern the software that runs their economic and civic life — not rent it from a company on another continent.

**Value should stay where it's created.** A euro spent in a neighborhood should circulate in that neighborhood, not get skimmed off to a distant balance sheet. Coordination creates enormous value. That value belongs to the people who generate it.

**Open by default.** Open source, forkable, no lock-in. If a community outgrows us or disagrees with us, they should be able to take the tool and leave. That isn't a weakness in the model. It is the point.

**Privacy is the baseline, not the upsell.** We do not build surveillance and sell it back to you as a feature. There is no data to monetize because we are not in that business.

**Start where people actually live.** We begin in Madrid, in real neighborhoods, with real shops and real neighbors and concrete problems. Not with abstract scale. The big picture is built from small, working things.

---

## What we're building

Software that lets small shops pool their buying power and negotiate as one. Cooperative alternatives to the platforms extracting from housing and tourism. Tools that make participatory budgeting work better, that surface where pressure is mounting in a city, that help communities map and share the resources they already have.

In every case the pattern is the same: the software enables the model — it never *becomes* the model. We are not building companies that own neighborhoods. We are building infrastructure that neighborhoods own.

---

## What we will not do

We will not become what we are replacing. We will not insert ourselves as the unavoidable toll booth and call it innovation. We will not ask permission from the system that profits from keeping us disconnected.

We will simply build the alternative — in the open, in public, in the places we live.

---

## Join us

If you run a shop, organize your neighbors, sit on a council, or write code — this is yours to take and build with. The technology to redistribute power already exists. What's been missing is the will to point it at the people instead of away from them.

The internet belongs to us. Let's act like it.
